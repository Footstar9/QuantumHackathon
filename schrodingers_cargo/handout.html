Schrödinger's |Cargo⟩

A two-player, pass-the-computer game about quantum error correction. One player is the noise of the universe. The other has to rescue a qubit they are not allowed to look at.

1 · How to play

Setup. Open index.html, type two names, pick the number of rounds (even, so both play each role equally) and the Noise budget (1 = tutorial, 2 = classic, 3 = chaos).

The cargo. Each round a random, secret qubit state is stored across 9 physical qubits in three crates. Nobody sees it.

Noise's turn (secret). Every qubit has four switches: I (leave alone), X bit flip, Z phase flip, Y both. Noise may touch at most budget qubits. Noise can see which lamps the errors will light, so they can try to set a trap. Then press Seal the cargo.

Pass the computer. A curtain screen hides everything. The Decoder presses "show my screen" only when Noise has looked away. The Noise player's choices are not on the Decoder's screen at all.

Decoder's turn. The Decoder sees only the 8 lamps. They flip switches to apply fixes; the lamps update to show what they would read after the fix. Press Ship it.

Scoring. If the cargo comes out exactly as it went in, the Decoder scores 1. Otherwise Noise scores 1. Roles swap every round. Highest score wins.

Reading the lamps. Orange lamps sit between two qubits of a crate and light when those two qubits' bits disagree, so they catch X and Y. Blue lamps sit between two crates and light when those crates' phases disagree, so they catch Z and Y. A lamp never tells you anything about the cargo itself.

Strategy tips

  • Decoder: start with the fewest errors that explain the lamps. That wins every time against a single error. Then ask: "what would a sneaky Noise do?"
  • Noise: two bit flips in one crate (say X on q1 and q2) light exactly the same lamp as one flip on q3. If the Decoder "fixes" q3, the three flips together silently flip the cargo.
  • Phase flips are forgiving: a Z on q1, q2 or q3 lights the same lamp, and fixing it with Z on any qubit in that crate works. You never need to know exactly which one.

2 · The quantum idea

What is being protected

The cargo is one qubit, α|0⟩ + β|1⟩. The amplitudes α and β are continuous and fragile. Real hardware suffers bit flips (X: |0⟩↔|1⟩), phase flips (Z: |+⟩↔|−⟩) and both at once (Y = iXZ). Any small error on a qubit can be written as a mix of I, X, Y and Z, and measuring the checks "snaps" it to one of them. So a code that fixes these four fixes every single-qubit error.

Why you can't just look

Classically you would copy the bit three times and peek at the copies. Quantum mechanics forbids both halves of that plan. The no-cloning theorem says you cannot copy an unknown state, and measuring a data qubit collapses the superposition and destroys α and β. So the Decoder in the game is never allowed to see the qubits, just like a real decoder.

Syndromes: asking only "do these agree?"

The trick is to measure joint properties instead of individual qubits. The 8 lamps are the 8 stabilizers of the code, Pauli operators that equal +1 on every valid cargo state:

LampOperatorQuestion it asksLights for
q1·q2, q2·q3 (and the same in crates B, C)Z₁Z₂, Z₂Z₃, Z₄Z₅, Z₅Z₆, Z₇Z₈, Z₈Z₉Do these two qubits have the same bit?X or Y on one of the two
phase A|B, phase B|CX₁…X₆, X₄…X₉Do these two crates have the same sign?Z or Y anywhere in those crates (odd number)

The answer to each question is the same for |0L⟩ and |1L⟩, so a measurement reveals the error (the syndrome) while revealing nothing about the cargo. On hardware each lamp is read using an extra helper (ancilla) qubit: CNOTs copy the parity onto the ancilla, which is then measured. That is exactly the circuit from Level 1 of the error-correction challenge. A lamp lights when the error anticommutes with that stabilizer.

The code: Shor's 9-qubit code

|0L⟩ = (|000⟩+|111⟩)⊗3/2√2    |1L⟩ = (|000⟩−|111⟩)⊗3/2√2

It is two repetition codes nested together. Inside each crate, three qubits form a bit-flip repetition code (orange lamps, Level 1 of the challenge). The three crates then form a phase-flip repetition code (blue lamps, Level 2: phase flips are bit flips seen through Hadamards). A Y lights both kinds (Level 3). The game turns the four challenge levels into one board.

What the lamps can and cannot tell apart

  • Every single error is fixable (27 of 27). The 8 lamps give 2⁸ = 256 patterns. A bit-flip part (X or Y) on any qubit lights its own unique orange pattern, and a phase-flip part (Z or Y) lights a unique blue pattern for its crate, so every one of the 27 single errors can be undone. The code has distance 3.
  • Some single errors look identical and that's fine. Z₁, Z₂, Z₃ light the same lamp. Fixing with Z₂ after a Z₁ leaves Z₁Z₂, which is a stabilizer: it does nothing to the cargo. This is called degeneracy, and it has no classical counterpart.
  • Two errors can impersonate one. X₁X₂ lights the same lamp as X₃. They differ by X₁X₂X₃, which flips the cargo's phase without lighting anything (a logical error). Likewise Z₁Z₄ looks like a single phase flip in crate C. With budget 2 the Decoder must guess which story is true.
  • The result is always one of three outcomes: leftover is a stabilizer (cargo safe), leftover is a logical X/Y/Z (lamps dark but cargo flipped), or lamps still lit (cargo knocked out of the code space).
How balanced is it? (computed by tests/test_qec.js) A "fewest-errors" decoder saves the cargo for 100% of budget-1 rounds, 66.8% of all possible budget-2 moves (235 of 352) and 40.7% of budget-3 moves, if Noise picked at random. Against two errors it saves 75% of double bit flips but only 25% of double phase flips, because phase errors across crates fool the blue lamps. A smart Noise does better than random and a smart Decoder adapts, which is what makes it a game.

3 · Motivation and originality

Error correction is the reason large quantum computers are possible at all, yet it is usually taught as tables of syndromes. We noticed that the decoder's real job is a guessing game against an opponent it cannot see, so we gave the opponent a face: a second player. Making noise adversarial turns abstract ideas into instincts. After a few rounds players discover, unprompted, why the fewest-errors rule works, why some different errors are "the same" (degeneracy), and how two small errors can combine into a silent logical error, the exact reason codes need larger distance.

What is distinctive: a pass-the-computer hidden-information format (no network, judges can play immediately), a board where the physical layout is the stabilizer structure, a live lamp preview that teaches "dark lamps are necessary but not sufficient", and a real state-vector simulation under the hood rather than a lookup table.

4 · How it was built and verified

FileRole
qec.jsThe quantum engine. A 9-qubit state-vector simulator (512 complex amplitudes) that encodes the cargo, applies Paulis exactly (including the i phases of Y) and measures each stabilizer as ⟨ψ|S|ψ⟩ = ±1. Plus a fast Pauli "bookkeeping" model (anticommutation counting) and the judgement that classifies the leftover error.
game.jsScreens, turns and scoring. The lamps the Decoder sees are measured on the simulated corrupted state. The final fidelities are computed by simulation too.
index.html, style.cssLayout and visuals. No libraries, no build step, no internet needed.
tests/test_qec.jsAutomated verification (below).

How a round is judged

Multiply Noise's error by the Decoder's fix to get the leftover Pauli R. If R anticommutes with any stabilizer, lamps are lit and the state is orthogonal to the original (fidelity 0). Otherwise R commutes with all stabilizers and is either a stabilizer (harmless) or a logical operator. We test that with the logicals X̄ = Z⊗9 and Z̄ = X⊗9: R acts as a logical X if it anticommutes with Z̄, as a logical Z if it anticommutes with X̄, and as Y if both.

Verification: node tests/test_qec.js → 45,529 checks pass

  1. The encoded states are normalised and orthogonal, every stabilizer reads +1 on 20 random cargos, and Z⊗9 and X⊗9 really act as logical X and Z.
  2. For every error on up to 3 qubits (2,620 errors) plus 3,000 random heavier ones, the lamps from direct simulation equal the bookkeeping syndrome table.
  3. For each of those errors, we encode the six test states |0⟩, |1⟩, |+⟩, |−⟩, |+i⟩, |−i⟩, apply the error, and check the simulated fidelity equals what the judgement predicts. "Safe" holds exactly when all six come back with fidelity 1, so the game protects quantum information, not just classical bits.
  4. All 27 single-qubit errors are fixed by a fewest-errors decoder.
  5. 400 full simulated rounds with random cargo, error and fix: fidelity is 1 whenever the game says "safe" and 0 whenever lamps are lit.

We also played full matches in a headless browser to check every screen and that no errors appear in the console.

5 · Limitations

  • Perfect checks. Real syndrome measurements are themselves noisy and are repeated many times. Here the lamps never lie.
  • Pauli errors only, chosen by a player. Real noise is random and continuous (small rotations, leakage, correlated errors). Pauli errors are the right model after syndrome measurement, but the game's "budget" is not a physical error rate.
  • Simulation, not hardware. The state is simulated classically (512 amplitudes is easy). The game does not show the ancilla circuits, only their outcomes.
  • Small code. The Shor code has distance 3, so two errors can already fool it. Modern hardware uses surface codes with thousands of qubits; the same game idea scales but would be much harder to play by hand.

6 · How to run

  1. Download the repository from GitHub (green Code button → Download ZIP, then unzip it), or git clone it.
  2. Open the schrodingers_cargo folder and double-click index.html. It opens in any modern browser. No install, no internet.
  3. To run the tests: install Node.js, then in a terminal inside schrodingers_cargo run node tests/test_qec.js.

7 · Demo script for the judges (about 4 minutes)

  1. Hook (20 s). "Quantum computers make constant errors, but you can't look at a qubit to check it, because looking destroys it. So how do you fix something you can't see? We turned that into a two-player game."
  2. Board (40 s). Start a game with budget 2. "One secret qubit is spread over nine physical qubits: Shor's code. Orange lamps ask 'do these two qubits agree on their bit?', blue lamps ask 'do these two crates agree on their phase?'. Those questions have the same answer whatever the cargo is, so they reveal the error without revealing the data."
  3. Easy round (40 s). We play Noise, put a single Z on q4, pass the laptop to a judge. Point at the cheat-sheet; the judge fixes it, often on a different qubit like q6. "You fixed a different qubit and it still worked. That leftover Z₄Z₆ is one of the code's own checks. Quantum codes are degenerate: you only need the right class of error."
  4. Trap round (60 s). Swap roles so the judge is Noise; or we play Noise again with X on q1 and q2. The Decoder sees one lamp, which looks like a single flip on q3. Fixing q3 turns all lamps dark… and the reveal says "logical Z". "All lamps dark is necessary, not sufficient. Two errors plus a fix made X₁X₂X₃, which flips the cargo's phase invisibly. That's why real codes need bigger distance."
  5. Verification (40 s). Run node tests/test_qec.js. "The lamps come from a real 512-amplitude state-vector simulation. The test checks every error on up to three qubits against the syndrome table and checks fidelity on the six standard test states: 45,529 checks, zero failures."
  6. Close (20 s). "Limitations: lamps never lie, noise is a player rather than physics, and it's simulated. But every lamp and every verdict in the game is genuine stabilizer-code physics."

8 · Questions the judges might ask

Why not just measure the qubits to see the errors?
Measuring a data qubit collapses α|0⟩+β|1⟩ and destroys the cargo, and you can't copy it first (no-cloning). Stabilizer measurements only reveal parities that are identical for |0L⟩ and |1L⟩.
Why 9 qubits and not 3 or 5?
3 qubits can fix bit flips or phase flips, not both. The 5-qubit code is the smallest that fixes both, but its checks (like XZZXI) are hard to read by eye. Shor's code is two repetition codes nested, so players can reason with "do these agree?" and it maps directly onto Levels 1 to 3 of the error-correction challenge.
Why does Y light both colours?
Y = iXZ: it has a bit-flip part (caught by the Z-type orange checks) and a phase-flip part (caught by the X-type blue checks).
Why does a single Z light a blue lamp but not tell you which qubit?
The blue checks act on whole crates (X on six qubits). Z₁, Z₂ and Z₃ all anticommute with X₁…X₆ in the same way. It doesn't matter: Z₁Z₂ is a stabilizer, so any Z in the right crate fixes it.
What happens with two errors?
The code has distance 3, so some pairs share a syndrome with a single error that differs from them by a logical operator. E.g. X₁X₂ vs X₃. No decoder can always win; with random moves the fewest-errors decoder wins 66.8% of budget-2 rounds.
How do you know the game's verdict is right?
The test suite simulates the actual 9-qubit state. It confirms the lamps match the stabilizer table for every error up to weight 3 (and 3,000 random heavier ones), and that "safe" holds exactly when the fidelity is 1 for all six cardinal states.
Where does the 'fidelity' number come from?
|⟨ψ|R|ψ⟩|², where R is the leftover error, computed on the simulated state. A logical Z leaves |0⟩ untouched (fidelity 1) but turns |+⟩ into |−⟩ (fidelity 0), which is why we also show the worst of the six test states.
How would you measure the lamps on real hardware?
For Z₁Z₂: CNOT q1→ancilla, CNOT q2→ancilla, measure the ancilla (our Level 1 circuit). For X₁…X₆: put the ancilla in |+⟩, CNOT from ancilla to each of the six qubits, Hadamard, measure.
What would you add next?
Noisy lamps (measurement errors, so the Decoder must repeat checks), a random-noise single-player mode with a computer decoder, and a surface-code board.
Did you use AI?
Yes, see below. We can explain and modify every part: for example, changing the CHECKS list in qec.js changes the code, and the tests immediately tell us whether the new lamp table still matches the simulation.
